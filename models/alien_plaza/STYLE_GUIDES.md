# Style guides, one per chapter

Why this file exists: a level looks good when one cohesive SET of pieces fills every slot on the screen. Weirdport works because its set is complete: a road texture, a sidewalk, a curb, walls with variation, a sky, trim on the edges, accents (AC units, trash, signs), and the living layer (trees, lamps, wires). Glowtide looked wrong because its set was improvised.

So every chapter gets the same checklist, filled with ITS OWN parts. The layout and routes never depend on the set, so a level can be greyboxed, made fun, and dressed last.

## The checklist (every chapter fills every line)

| Slot | What it covers | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Ground A | the main walking surface (road, paving, grass) | the biggest area on screen |
| Ground B | the second surface (sidewalk, sand, dirt) | breaks the first one up |
| Edge | curb, quay lip, ledge, roof rim | where two surfaces meet; the eye follows edges |
| Wall | 2 to 4 wall materials in ONE palette, plus one base course | the second biggest area on screen |
| Trim | window frames, cornices, door frames, posts | separates wall from roof and ground; reads as "built" |
| Roof | the roof surface and its rim | seen from above on every rooftop route |
| Openings | windows, doors, shutters, awnings | the rhythm of a facade |
| Accents | 4 to 6 small props that mean this place | the things the eye lands on at human height |
| Living | trees, bushes, grass, weeds, lamps, wires, banners | makes it feel inhabited |
| Sky + light | the sky dome and the sun colour | the mood; also the only thing behind everything |
| Signature effect | ONE effect the level is known for | water, smoke, blowing grass, dust, fog |

Rule: a new piece is rendered next to a reference shot before it ships. If it does not hold up beside the reference, it does not go in.

## How a chapter's set gets made

1. Greybox the level and prove its routes (the checker).
2. Pick the reference shots (three or four images that are the mood).
3. Fill the checklist above for the chapter.
4. Make the ground, edge, wall and sky first; render against the reference; fix until it holds up.
5. Then trim, roof and openings. Then accents and living. Then the signature effect.
6. Only then dress the level.

Glowtide is at step 1 with a borrowed set (the city's) to test the layout. Its own set starts at step 2.

## Composition: every waypoint is a shot

The reference is the Sites painting from the hotel: simple shapes, three bands, a path that leads the eye, five percent loud colour. The level is seen from where the player stands, so every intended waypoint (A to B to C) is a camera, looking at the next waypoint, and each frame has to pass:

1. **Three value bands.** Foreground dark and big, mid ground the lightest (the sand, the paving), background a different hue (the green hill, then sky). If the top, middle and bottom thirds of the frame have the same brightness, the shot is flat.
2. **A big foreground shape.** Something large and partly cut off by the frame edge in the bottom third: a tree, a wall corner, a railing, a bush. Without it the frame is a postcard, not a place you stand in.
3. **The landmark on a thirds point.** The next door (clock tower, gate, lighthouse) sits near one of the four thirds intersections, never dead centre and never at the edge.
4. **70 / 25 / 5 colour.** Most of the frame quiet (sand, plaster, sage), a quarter mid (greens, blues), a twentieth loud (red, orange, pink). The loud bits go on the thirds lines and on the things that matter: the core, the door, the bell.
5. **Big, medium, small.** Two or three huge shapes, a handful medium, many tiny. Never ten of the same size.
6. **A path leads in** from a bottom corner toward the landmark: a road, a quay edge, a railing, a line of lamps.

`compose.py` renders every waypoint shot and scores 1 to 4 automatically (5 and 6 are by eye). A shot marked FIX is where dressing goes next: a foreground tree, a loud accent, a darker base. Dressing goes where a shot needs it, not everywhere.

## The building formula (from his Portland walk: the white brick warehouse, the modern brick block, the low-poly poster)

A building is never a box with a texture on it. Reading the real ones, every one of them, old or new, cheap or dear, has the same seven parts, and the low-poly poster building proves they survive with almost no polygons:

1. **Plinth.** A base course at the foot, 0.6 to 1.2 m, a different material (concrete under brick, stone under plaster), slightly proud. It takes the dirt and the knocks.
2. **String course at every floor.** A thin band or slab edge where each floor meets the next. The poster building is literally slabs with walls between them.
3. **Sill under every window,** proud by a hand's width. The poster's pink sills are the whole charm of it.
4. **Lintel or soldier course over every opening.** Bricks on end, or a flat band.
5. **Pilasters.** Vertical strips that divide a long wall into bays; the recessed panels between them are where windows go, or where windows WERE (bricked-in windows are the best imperfection in the set).
6. **Cornice and parapet.** Corbelled courses stepping out at the top, then a coping. The roof slab overhangs on the poster building.
7. **Roof furniture.** A stack, a hatch, a tank, a vent. One or two, never none.

Then the imperfections that make it a place: a bricked-up window, a downpipe, a utility box bolted on, a roll-up shutter with its drum above it, paint that has peeled back to brick, a crack with a strap repair. One or two per building, placed where the eye lands.

The kit's walls already carry 2, 3 and 4. `kit_hf` adds 1, 2 (string courses), 6 (cornice band and coping) to every building in the level, and rooftop dressing adds 7. Still to do: pilasters on long walls, and the imperfections as decals.

## Three of everything (the kit rule)

A kit is not a hundred pieces; it is THREE of each thing, and the combinations do the rest. Three corners, three awnings, three roofs, three bricks, two or three doors, three windows, three parapets, three shop fronts. Each one is a different IDEA, not a recolour: the steel canopy with slats, the straight fabric awning with a valance, the curved drape with a scalloped front. Then colour and texture multiply them.

And the massing: a building is not one rectangle. The upper floors jog left of the lower ones, or the middle third of a long front steps out half a metre, or the corner is cut, or the top floor is set back behind a terrace. Subtle: half a metre, one bay. It breaks the straight line from every angle and gives the formula (plinth, string course, cornice) corners to turn.
